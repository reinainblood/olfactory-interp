The cookie problem

In an earlier experiment, a model walking through an imaginary Costco shopped differently after activations from a sentence about cookies were inserted into its prompt. A shopping cart gives us an action to score. How much of the original sentence came along for the ride?

In an audit of that earlier study, a nearest-match search at layer 71 recovered the exact donor sentence for 47 of 47 candidate cases in the first run. The model could have relied on information specific to the wording. The question for this weekend is whether a cue assembled from Kimi’s own odor comparisons can carry information about an unnamed sample into a new decision.

Finding the answer after Kimi thinks

For each scored case, Kimi can reason until it ends its turn naturally. The measurement is the relative score for A versus B at the first position where Kimi can give its final answer. On 12 explicit controls, it chose the designated answer 12 times and its native logit difference favored that answer 12 times.

We then audited the first hundred scientific completions in order. Of these, 32 recorded internal activations only and 14 were held-out reference judgments. Among the other 54, the native A−B score was available in 54 of 54. The sampled letter occupied the first final-answer position in 54. This is an empirical check that the instrument is reading the intended moment.

What would count as odor-specific evidence?

The writer is built from how Kimi groups named samples by smell. In a later held-out trial the model sees a neutral carrier and two reference choices. The experimenter changes the carrier’s internal activation during the forward pass. All four paired runs receive the same input tokens and seed. Their hidden conditions supply the intended odor profile, a printed-name control, the wrong odor profile, or a sham.

An odor-specific effect should favor the unnamed sample’s previously recorded native preference more strongly than those competing explanations. Selective deletion and, where justified, restoration test whether the same component participates in ordinary named judgments. A separate household task asks whether a cue can retrieve an arbitrary detail from an earlier visit.

At this checkpoint the scientific odor-write comparison is pending. A reliable positive result would justify testing a mapping from physical sensor signals into this representation. Subjective smell, the training stage at which it arose, and long-term memory across sessions each require evidence beyond this task.
