Can the cue identify a molecule under a different name?

In Kimi, a trace of the compound survives a change of name. Spelling predicts more matches. Methyl octanoate shows where the two measurements part company.

For each of 24 fitting compounds, Kimi read a sentence about the character of its odor and another about the shape of its printed name. Both frames were repeated with a chemical alias. At the neutral marker, we subtracted the printed-name state from the odor state. Could that difference find the same compound among all 24 aliases? We also ran the search in reverse. These are 48 directional queries reusing 24 identities.

The frame difference identified the correct molecule in 10, 12, 13 of 48 queries at L23, L47 and L71, respectively. Its advantage over the printed-name frame was inconsistent. Chemical identity remains recoverable after the subtraction, and ordinary spelling remains a stronger full-gallery identifier in this dataset.

Matched foils, permutation evidence and acquisition limits

Against a single rival, the frame-difference signal favored the correct alias 79.2%, 75.0%, 75.0% of the time at L23, L47 and L71. Only 4 compounds had another eligible member of the same frozen family. The other 20 used the nearest competing molecular fingerprint. There were 14 distinct foil identities; these pair results are dependent.

We shuffled the correspondence between molecule identities 10,000 times, carrying alias directions together and using the same permutations for each view and layer. For the frame difference against random identity assignment, one-sided plus-one permutation p values were 0.000600, 0.000100, 0.000100. For its paired top-1 difference from the printed-name frame (-8.33, +6.25, +0.00 percentage points), the respective upper-tail shuffle references were 0.9824, 0.0703, 0.6676. These are descriptive, unadjusted comparisons across layers, views, galleries and contrasts. A small identity-shuffle probability tests recovery of molecular identity, not the presence of a sensation.
